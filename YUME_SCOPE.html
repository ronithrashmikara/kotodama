Visko Orbis Challenge · Scope & Status

Yume 夢

A Japanese-learning game where the only way to change the world in front of you is to say it in Japanese — built on a live, steerable video model.

Ronit Rashmikara · deadline 1 October 2026 · repository ronithrashmikara/yume

1. The idea in one paragraph

Most language apps test recognition — pick the right answer from four. Yume tests expression, and then shows you the consequence. You are dropped into a living, AI-generated world. It narrates itself to you in Japanese. When you say something back and the meaning lands, the live video steers to match within seconds. When it does not land, nothing moves. The video itself is the feedback.

The name comes from kotodama (言霊), the old belief that spoken words carry the power to shape reality. That metaphor is the product.

2. Why this needs a live model

This is the highest-weighted judging criterion, so it is worth stating precisely. Two properties make pre-rendered video impossible here:

  • The next sentence is unpredictable. A learner can say anything, at any level, in any order. There is no finite set of clips to pre-generate.
  • The world keeps moving when you don't. Every ~22 seconds the scene changes on its own, and every third change asks something of you. A clip cannot keep going while you hesitate.

The second property is the stronger argument, and it is also nearly free: Orbis bills for every wall-clock second the GPU is held whether the scene changes or not. A static scene is wasted spend. An evolving one costs the same.

4. How it fits together

You say it

Japanese, spoken or typed. Always-on recognition — nothing to press.

→
Understand

GPT-OSS 120B on Groq. Grades meaning, writes Hina's reply, drifts the world. ~1.4 s.

→
Steer the world

Reactor SDK → Visko Orbis over WebRTC. The running scene changes; no new clip.

↺ the changed world narrates itself back in Japanese, and the loop restarts

Supporting layers

  • Fish Audio — speaks narration and Hina, in Japanese and English.
  • Next.js 16 / React 19 / TypeScript — app shell and five API routes (check-answer, narrate, drift, companion, tts).
  • localStorage — saved vocabulary and SRS schedule. No account, no database.
  • fal.ai — all backgrounds, the favicon, the mascot, and the presentation artwork. Not the live-video engine.

Two design decisions worth defending

The scene is an event log, not a growing string

Each change is recorded as an event with its source. One structure produces both the Orbis prompt and the end-of-run story, and it caps how many recent beats reach the prompt — without that cap, a world drifting every 22 s would grow the prompt without bound and the earliest details would compete with the newest for the model's attention.

Narration arrives pre-segmented from the server

Japanese has no spaces, so making every word individually hoverable normally means shipping a morphological analyser (~15 MB) to the browser. Instead the model returns the sentence already split into units with readings and glosses, and the concatenation is validated against the original.

Three cost traps

Idle bills at full rate. Reactor meters every wall-clock second the GPU is held, "even if you are idle". Billing granularity is ambiguous — docs say per session-minute, the pricing API quotes per-second; assume short aborted takes round up. Sessions hard-stop at 300 s, so a take running past 5:00 loses the world mid-shot. The in-HUD meter turns amber at 4:00 for exactly this reason.

7. What we could build next

Ordered by judging impact per hour of work. The real-time story is already strong, so the marginal value of more mechanics is lower than the marginal value of a convincing demo — but there is room for both.

Barge-in · ~2 h

Interrupt Hina mid-sentence and she stops and listens. We already track when she is speaking, so the machinery exists. This is the single change that most makes her feel alive rather than turn-based.

Silence she fills · ~2 h

Say nothing for ~15 s and she prompts you — どうしたの？ — or points something out. A conversation that survives hesitation reads as genuinely live on camera.

Listening comprehension · ~3 h

She asks you to do something and the world only moves if you understood her. The app currently tests production only; this adds the input half, which is what actually builds fluency.

Pronunciation feedback · ~3 h

Web Speech returns a confidence score per result. Low confidence on a word you meant to say is a mispronunciation signal she can gently echo back. Genuinely hard for a textbook to do.

Two more worlds · ~2 h

Cheap and safe. Makes the app feel fuller on camera and demonstrates that the scenario format generalises.

Shareable story card · ~2 h

Render the end-of-run story log as an image. Turns a session into something a learner would post, which is the cheapest distribution a language app can have.

8. Risks and open questions

Hina's visual consistency is unproven

The headline feature rests on Orbis honouring a reference frame across repeated set_prompt calls. Both mitigations are implemented (image conditioning + the locked description restated every steer), but if she visibly becomes a different person after a few turns, the companion demo needs rethinking. Two minutes of browser testing resolves this.

The repository is private

Judges cannot read the code. Git history has been scanned and contains no committed secrets, so it is safe to publish — but it must actually be flipped before submission.

Groq free-tier limits during a long demo

Living-world drift plus narration is roughly 5–6 calls per minute. Comfortable against 30 RPM, but a long unattended session could approach the 1,000/day cap.

Everything is single-player and local

Vocabulary lives in localStorage: per-browser, no account, no sync. Correct for a hackathon prototype, and worth saying out loud rather than implying a backend exists.
